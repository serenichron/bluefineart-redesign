# Floors, walls and ceilings of real galleries (2026-11-10)

Why this exists: three room designs missed. The last one looked like a cheap video game. This note looks only at the three surfaces of a gallery room, and at how real galleries use colour.

## How I researched
- Text sources for four well-known gallery buildings, listed at the end.
- Photos I looked at earlier: nine of Blue Gallery, plus Kasmin and Xavier Hufkens.
- This round I could not get clean photos of the four buildings. Cookie banners and slow image loading got in the way. So the notes on them come from written descriptions, not from my own eyes.

## The pattern

**Floor.** One material, wall to wall, with no pattern. It is concrete, pale timber or terrazzo. It is matte or satin, never glossy. It is a mid tone, darker than the walls, so the room feels grounded.

**Walls.** Flat and plain. No skirting board: the wall stops just above the floor, and a thin dark gap shows. Corners are sharp. The wall is a background and nothing else.

**Ceiling.** This is where every one of these buildings spends its effort. The ceiling is a light source. Daylight comes through glass, fabric or deep coffers, so it arrives soft and even. The ceiling has depth and rhythm, and that is the "something special overhead".

**What makes them rich.** Proportion, one honest material per surface, and soft light with real shadows. Decoration and bright colour play no part.

## Colour on gallery walls
- White walls became the rule at MoMA in the 1930s. Before that, coloured walls were normal.
- Museums are moving back to colour. London's National Gallery used greyish green as its neutral, then reds, and in 2025 hung Renaissance works on dark blue.
- The colours that work are muted. A colour consultant who worked with the Met and the Getty put it this way: you should not notice the wall colour behind a picture.
- Mid-tone greys with a blue-green cast are said to hold very different works together.
- A dark wall makes a painting look lighter. A wall in the opposite colour to the painting makes the painting's colour stronger.

What Alex asked for has a name: muted, or greyed, colour. It is a colour with grey mixed in, so it stays soft even when it is dark.

## What went wrong in "Arches and colour"
- Three saturated colours plus gold. Real galleries use one muted colour at most.
- Shapes as decoration: arches and a ribbon.
- A patterned floor.
- No real light. Every surface was a flat picture, and nothing cast a shadow.

## The room I propose
- **Floor:** pale grey polished concrete. One surface, satin, with a faint reflection.
- **Walls:** warm white, flat, with a shadow gap at the floor.
- **One coloured wall:** the far wall, in a muted tone. A second, quieter tone on the floating wall. That is the colour play.
- **Ceiling:** a daylight ceiling. Deep white coffers with glowing panels, so light falls soft and even.
- **Light:** computed, not painted. A script works out how light from the ceiling falls, bounces and leaves shadows. The coloured wall will tint the floor near it, as it would in life.
- **Nothing else:** one bench. No arches, no ribbon, no statues.
